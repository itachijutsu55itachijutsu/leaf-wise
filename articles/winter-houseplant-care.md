# Winter Houseplant Care: How to Keep Your Indoor Plants Healthy Through the Cold Months

Last winter, I watched two plants I'd cared for over four years slowly turn yellow. One was my money plant, growing in water inside the house. The other was my aloe vera, which I kept outside where it received sunlight. Their leaves were losing color little by little, and I was worried I might lose both of them. After keeping them for so long, seeing them deteriorate was genuinely upsetting.

What confused me was that they lived in completely different places. My money plant was in the hall, away from direct sunlight, while the aloe vera was outside in the sun. Yet both seemed to be struggling. I didn't know whether the problem was light, water or something else, so I called a nursery specialist rather than continuing to guess.

He advised me to change the money plant's water completely, put its roots into fresh water in a clean glass container, and treat the leaves with a spray. For the aloe vera, he advised daily watering. I followed his instructions, and within three to four days both plants began looking better. It took around a month for them to recover fully.

That experience taught me something more useful than any fixed winter watering schedule: **two plants showing similar symptoms can need very different care.** I also wouldn't recommend daily watering for every aloe vera plant simply because it was the advice given for mine. Aloe usually needs well-draining soil and careful watering, especially in cooler weather. I don't know exactly which factor caused the yellowing or which part of the treatment helped most.

Winter changes the conditions around houseplants. Days get shorter, natural light often becomes less useful, windows get colder and heated rooms may become dry. Many plants slow down, and the care routine that worked in warmer months may stop being suitable.

Here's how to adjust watering, light, humidity and placement during winter, and what to check before a yellow leaf turns into a bigger problem.

## Why Houseplants Struggle During Winter

Most popular houseplants originally come from tropical or subtropical environments. They're accustomed to relatively stable temperatures, consistent light and, in many cases, higher humidity than a heated home provides during winter.

A typical home offers very different conditions between July and January.

Four changes are especially important.

### 1. Your Plants Receive Less Sunlight

During winter, daylight hours decrease and the sun follows a lower path across the sky.

Even a plant sitting directly beside a window may receive considerably less usable light than it did during summer.

Light drives photosynthesis, the process plants use to produce sugars that support growth and maintenance.

When light becomes limited, photosynthesis slows. Many houseplants produce fewer leaves, grow more slowly or temporarily stop producing visible new growth.

The University of Illinois Extension identifies insufficient light as a major challenge for indoor plants during winter.

### 2. The Soil May Stay Wet Longer

Reduced growth usually means reduced water use.

When a plant receives less light, it often draws water from the soil more slowly. If the room is cool, evaporation from the potting mix may also decrease.

The result is soil that remains damp longer than expected.

This becomes particularly problematic when someone continues watering according to their summer routine.

Roots need oxygen as well as moisture. Soil that remains saturated can limit oxygen around the roots and encourage conditions associated with root rot.

### 3. Indoor Heating Reduces Humidity

Heating systems warm indoor air without necessarily adding moisture.

As the temperature rises, relative humidity can fall, leaving the environment considerably drier.

This is particularly noticeable in homes using central heating, radiators or forced-air heating systems.

Plants that prefer humid environments may develop crispy leaf edges, curled leaves or dry tips.

Ferns, calatheas and certain palms are particularly sensitive.

### 4. Temperatures Become Less Consistent

A living room might feel comfortable in the middle, while the windowsill becomes cold enough at night to damage sensitive foliage.

Meanwhile, a plant near a heating vent may experience hot, dry air several times a day.

These changes matter because many indoor plants grow best within a fairly stable temperature range.

A location that worked beautifully throughout summer may no longer be suitable.

## How Often Should You Water Houseplants in Winter?

Watering is where many winter houseplant problems begin.

A plant with yellow leaves or drooping stems looks thirsty, so adding more water seems reasonable.

Unfortunately, overwatered plants can show many of the same symptoms as underwatered plants.

When roots remain in poorly aerated, wet soil, they may become damaged and lose their ability to absorb water effectively. The leaves can then wilt even though moisture is readily available in the pot.

That's why **you should check the soil before deciding whether a plant needs water.**

### What Happened With My Aloe Vera

The nursery specialist I contacted advised me to water my yellowing aloe vera daily. I followed that advice for my particular plant, and it started showing improvement within three to four days. By about a month later, it had recovered.

I'm sharing that because it was part of what actually happened, **not because daily watering is the right routine for aloe vera in winter**. Aloe is a succulent, and constantly wet soil can damage its roots. Without knowing the exact weather, soil moisture, drainage and root condition at the time, I can't say why that recommendation worked in my situation. If your aloe is turning yellow, check its soil and roots before increasing watering, and seek plant-specific advice if the cause isn't clear.

### Stop Following a Fixed Watering Schedule

Watering every Sunday might work during the warmer months, but it becomes unreliable when your plants are growing slowly.

Instead, check the potting mix.

For many common tropical houseplants, you can insert a finger approximately one or two inches into the soil.

If the upper layer still feels damp, watering can usually wait.

If it feels dry, consider the plant's specific moisture requirements before watering.

This distinction matters because not every plant should dry out to the same depth.

Peace lilies generally prefer more consistent moisture than snake plants, for example.

A succulent may need the soil to dry much more thoroughly before receiving additional water.

### Learn What Your Pot Feels Like When It's Dry

Another useful method is checking the weight of the pot.

Lift it shortly after watering and notice how heavy it feels. Then check again as the soil gradually dries.

After a few cycles, you'll have a much better sense of when that particular plant needs attention.

This is especially helpful with deeper pots, where the surface may appear dry while the soil around the roots remains wet.

Moisture meters can also be useful, although inexpensive models are not always reliable across different potting mixes.

### Water Thoroughly, But Less Frequently

Reducing watering frequency doesn't mean giving your plant only a few drops.

For plants that need watering, it's generally better to moisten the root zone thoroughly and allow excess water to drain through the bottom of the pot.

Then empty the saucer.

Leaving a pot standing in collected water can keep the lower roots unnecessarily wet.

Use water around room temperature when possible, particularly for tropical plants kept in warm rooms.

### How Different Plants Respond to Winter Watering

| Plant | Winter watering approach |
|---|---|
| Snake plant | Allow the soil to dry thoroughly |
| ZZ plant | Water sparingly after substantial drying |
| Pothos | Let the upper soil dry before watering |
| Peace lily | Maintain moderate moisture without saturation |
| Monstera | Reduce frequency as growth slows |
| Succulents | Allow significant drying between waterings |
| Ferns | Maintain suitable moisture without waterlogging |

These are general starting points, not fixed schedules.

A snake plant in a cool, dim room may go several weeks without needing water. The same plant in a bright, warm room might dry faster.

**The soil and the plant's condition matter more than the calendar.**

If the soil constantly stays wet and small insects begin flying around the pot, read our [guide to fungus gnats](/fungus-gnats-in-houseplants/).

If a plant continues wilting despite damp soil, our [root rot guide](/root-rot-in-houseplants/) explains what to inspect.

## Give Your Houseplants More Light Without Damaging Them

Winter light can be surprisingly weak.

A window that provided plenty of light in summer might receive only a few hours of direct sunlight during the colder months.

Before buying new equipment, examine how much natural light reaches your plants.

### Move Plants Closer to Suitable Windows

In the Northern Hemisphere, south-facing windows generally receive useful winter sunlight, although nearby buildings, trees and window coverings can make a significant difference.

East-facing windows may work well for plants that appreciate gentle morning sunlight.

West-facing windows can also provide valuable afternoon light.

However, moving a plant directly against cold glass creates another problem.

Leaves touching chilled windowpanes may suffer cold damage, especially during freezing weather.

Keep some space between foliage and the glass.

If your windows become extremely cold overnight, move sensitive plants slightly farther into the room.

### My Money Plant Was Far From Direct Sunlight

My money plant was kept in the hall, in front of a room, away from direct sunlight. When its leaves started turning yellow, I initially had no clear explanation. At the same time, my aloe vera was outside receiving sun and was also yellowing. That was a reminder that sunlight alone doesn't tell you what is wrong with a plant.

The nursery specialist did not tell me to move the money plant. Instead, he changed its old water, placed its roots in fresh water in a glass container and used a leaf spray. I kept it in the same general location. It began to improve within a few days, and its recovery took about a month.

Because several things changed at once, I can't attribute the recovery to the fresh water, the spray or any single step. If you grow a money plant in water, checking water cleanliness, root condition and available indirect light is a sensible place to start. Don't copy an unidentified spray treatment without knowing what it contains and whether it's suitable for your plant.

### Remove Anything Blocking Light

Small improvements can help when sunlight is limited.

Wipe dust from large leaves with a soft, damp cloth. Clean windows when necessary, and open curtains during daylight hours.

Rotate pots occasionally if the plant grows strongly toward one side.

Be careful with plants that dislike frequent disturbance. You don't need to reposition everything every few days.

### When Does a Grow Light Make Sense?

Not every houseplant needs artificial lighting.

A snake plant may tolerate a dim location reasonably well, although it will usually grow better with appropriate light.

A fiddle leaf fig or indoor citrus plant may struggle considerably more in the same conditions.

If your home receives very little natural sunlight, a dedicated LED grow light can help maintain healthier growth.

Choose a light intended for growing plants rather than assuming any bright household bulb will work equally well.

Follow the manufacturer's recommendations for placement and distance.

Many indoor growing setups use approximately 10–14 hours of artificial light daily, but the suitable duration depends on the plant, light intensity and available natural light.

A timer helps maintain a consistent schedule.

Keep an eye on the leaves after installing a grow light.

Bleaching, excessive heat or unusual discoloration may indicate the light is too intense or too close.

For more specific requirements, see our [snake plant care guide](/snake-plant-care/), [pothos guide](/pothos-care-and-propagation/) and [fiddle leaf fig care guide](/fiddle-leaf-fig-care/).

## Protect Your Plants From Cold Windows and Heating Vents

You don't necessarily need to keep your entire home warmer for your plants.

A better approach is identifying locations with excessive temperature changes.

Many common tropical houseplants prefer indoor temperatures around 65–75°F (18–24°C), although individual species differ.

Some tolerate cooler temperatures, while others develop damage after relatively brief exposure to cold.

### Check Your Windowsills at Night

A windowsill can be a useful place during the day and an uncomfortable one after sunset.

Cold glass, poorly sealed frames and outdoor drafts can significantly reduce the temperature around nearby foliage.

If your plant suddenly develops dark, soft patches after a particularly cold night, temperature damage is one possibility.

Move sensitive plants away from freezing windows.

Avoid allowing leaves to press directly against the glass.

### Keep Plants Away From Direct Heating

Radiators and heating vents create the opposite problem.

A plant positioned directly above a radiator may experience repeated bursts of hot, dry air.

The soil can dry unevenly, leaves may lose moisture quickly and sensitive plants may develop brown edges.

Moving the pot a short distance away from the heat source can sometimes resolve the problem without any additional equipment.

### Avoid Drafty Doorways

A tropical plant placed beside an entrance may be exposed to sudden cold air whenever the door opens.

One brief exposure might not cause noticeable damage, but repeated cold drafts can stress susceptible plants.

Think about the actual conditions around the pot, not just the temperature shown on your room thermostat.

## What to Do About Dry Indoor Air

Humidity becomes a bigger concern during winter, particularly in heated homes.

But there's an important distinction:

**Brown leaf tips don't automatically mean the humidity is too low.**

Inconsistent watering, excess fertilizer salts, root problems and other environmental stressors can produce similar symptoms.

Before buying a humidifier, check the basics.

Is your plant close to a radiator? Does the soil dry out unusually quickly? Are the roots healthy?

If those conditions are under control, low humidity becomes a stronger possibility.

### Measure Humidity Instead of Guessing

An inexpensive digital hygrometer can show the relative humidity around your plants.

Many commonly grown tropical houseplants appreciate moderate humidity, often around 40–60%, although requirements vary.

You don't need to turn your living room into a greenhouse.

The goal is to provide conditions appropriate for the species while avoiding excessive indoor moisture.

Very high humidity can encourage condensation and mold problems, especially in poorly ventilated rooms.

### A Humidifier Is Usually More Effective Than Misting

Misting leaves may briefly increase surface moisture, but the effect on surrounding room humidity is generally short-lived.

A properly maintained humidifier can provide a more consistent improvement.

Place it where humidity-loving plants benefit without soaking nearby walls, furniture or electrical equipment.

Clean the humidifier according to its instructions to prevent microorganisms and mineral deposits from accumulating.

### Does Grouping Plants Together Help?

Grouping plants may create a slightly more humid microclimate as moisture evaporates from their soil and leaves.

However, the effect is usually limited in a large, dry room.

It can still be helpful, provided the plants have adequate airflow and aren't crowded so tightly that pests and disease become harder to detect.

## Should You Fertilize Houseplants During Winter?

For most houseplants growing slowly in low winter light, regular fertilizing is unnecessary.

Plants use nutrients to support growth and normal biological processes.

When growth slows considerably, their nutrient requirements usually decrease.

Continuing the same feeding schedule from summer may cause soluble fertilizer salts to accumulate in the potting mix.

Excess salts can interfere with water uptake and contribute to root damage or brown leaf margins.

The University of Connecticut Extension advises reducing or avoiding winter fertilization when houseplants are inactive because of lower light and cooler temperatures.

### When Should You Start Feeding Again?

Watch for active growth rather than relying only on the month.

Fresh leaves, developing shoots and other signs of healthy growth suggest the plant is becoming more active.

For many houseplants in temperate Northern Hemisphere locations, this happens as daylight increases in spring.

However, plants kept under effective grow lights in consistently warm conditions may continue growing through winter.

Some flowering houseplants also have different feeding requirements.

Certain orchids and winter-flowering plants may benefit from fertilizer during their active growth or flowering cycles.

Always consider the species and its current condition.

If you're unsure about fertilizer strength or frequency, our [houseplant fertilizing guide](/how-to-fertilize-houseplants/) covers the basics.

## Is Winter a Bad Time to Repot Houseplants?

Generally, spring is a more convenient time for routine repotting because many plants are entering a period of stronger growth.

That doesn't mean repotting in winter is automatically harmful.

The real question is whether the plant needs it.

If your houseplant is healthy and simply looks a little less active, there's usually no reason to disturb the roots.

Repotting changes the root environment and can temporarily stress the plant, especially if you damage roots or move it into a much larger pot.

A bigger pot also holds more growing medium, which may remain wet longer if the plant isn't using much water.

### When You Shouldn't Wait Until Spring

Some situations need attention regardless of the season.

If roots are rotting, the potting mix is severely damaged or the container has broken, waiting several months could make matters worse.

In these cases, appropriate repotting may be necessary.

Choose a suitable potting mix and avoid increasing the pot size unnecessarily.

Our [repotting houseplants guide](/how-to-repot-a-houseplant/) explains how to assess when a new container is actually needed.

## Winter Houseplant Pests: What to Look For

Indoor pests don't disappear just because the weather outside becomes cold.

In fact, heated indoor environments can be favorable for certain pests, especially spider mites.

Plants that are already stressed may also be less resilient when an infestation develops.

The good news is that early detection makes pest management considerably easier.

### Inspect Your Plants Every Week

When checking soil moisture, spend another minute looking closely at the leaves.

Pay particular attention to the undersides, where small pests may remain unnoticed.

Look for:

- Fine webbing or tiny moving specks, which may indicate spider mites.
- White, cotton-like clusters associated with mealybugs.
- Small, attached bumps that could be scale insects.
- Sticky residue, distorted new growth or unexplained leaf discoloration.

Finding one yellow leaf doesn't automatically mean your plant has pests.

Look for a pattern and inspect carefully.

### What If You Find an Infestation?

Move the affected plant away from nearby houseplants to reduce the chance of pests spreading.

Identify the pest before choosing a treatment.

Different infestations require different approaches, and applying products without knowing the cause can waste time or damage sensitive leaves.

Our [houseplant pest identification guide](/houseplant-pests-spider-mites-mealybugs-scale/) explains how to recognize spider mites, mealybugs and scale insects.

## Be Careful With Holiday Plants Around Pets and Children

Winter brings seasonal decorative plants into many homes.

Poinsettias, amaryllis, holly and mistletoe are common examples.

Their safety varies, and some can cause significant problems if eaten.

Poinsettias, for example, are generally associated with irritation and mild gastrointestinal symptoms, while certain other seasonal plants can be considerably more dangerous.

Don't assume a plant is safe simply because it's commonly sold as a holiday decoration.

Before bringing a new plant home, look it up using the [ASPCA's toxic and non-toxic plant database](https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants).

Keep potentially harmful plants away from children and animals.

Our [pet-safe houseplant guide](/pet-safe-houseplants/) also includes alternatives worth considering.

## Winter Care for Different Types of Houseplants

A single winter watering or humidity rule won't work equally well for every species.

Here are some practical differences worth understanding.

### Snake Plants and ZZ Plants

These plants generally tolerate periods of dry soil better than prolonged saturation.

Allow substantial drying between waterings, particularly when they're kept in low light.

They don't need constant misting or unusually high humidity in most homes.

### Pothos and Philodendrons

Both are relatively adaptable, but reduced winter light may slow their growth.

Water when the potting mix reaches an appropriate level of dryness.

Avoid aggressive pruning simply because the vines haven't produced new leaves recently.

### Peace Lilies

Peace lilies prefer more consistent moisture, but they still need oxygen around their roots.

Don't allow the soil to remain saturated.

Keep them away from cold drafts and excessive heating.

If a peace lily starts drooping, inspect the soil before assuming it's thirsty.

See our [peace lily care guide](/peace-lily-care-and-drooping/) for more details.

### Monsteras

Monsteras generally appreciate bright, indirect light.

In winter, they may need a brighter position than they did during summer.

Because growth often slows, reduce watering frequency according to the soil's drying rate.

Our [Monstera care guide](/monstera-care/) covers light, moisture and other growing requirements.

### Fiddle Leaf Figs

Fiddle leaf figs can be sensitive to sudden changes in their environment.

Insufficient light, cold drafts and irregular watering may contribute to leaf problems.

Find a stable, suitably bright position and avoid repeatedly moving the plant without a reason.

### Orchids

Orchid requirements depend heavily on the type.

Many popular Phalaenopsis orchids prefer bright, indirect light and careful watering.

Their roots shouldn't remain constantly waterlogged.

Some orchids benefit from seasonal temperature differences, while others have very different growing and flowering cycles.

See our [beginner orchid guide](/orchid-care-for-beginners/) for species-specific advice.

### Chinese Money Plants

Pilea peperomioides generally prefers bright, indirect light.

Rotate the pot occasionally to encourage balanced growth, and allow the potting mix to dry appropriately between waterings.

Our [Chinese money plant guide](/chinese-money-plant-care/) explains its care in more detail.

## How to Identify Common Winter Houseplant Problems

Winter plant problems can look similar on the surface, so it's worth checking several possible causes instead of immediately adding water or fertilizer.

| Symptom | Possible causes | What to check first |
|---|---|---|
| Yellow leaves | Excess watering, low light, normal leaf aging | Soil moisture and drainage |
| Drooping leaves | Dry soil, damaged roots, temperature stress | Soil condition and root health |
| Brown, crispy tips | Dry air, inconsistent watering, fertilizer salts | Humidity and watering history |
| Sudden leaf drop | Cold drafts, environmental change, root stress | Recent changes in location |
| Long, weak stems | Insufficient light | Window exposure and light levels |
| Soft stems near soil | Rot or other tissue damage | Roots, drainage and stem base |
| Small flying insects | Often fungus gnats | Persistent moisture in potting mix |

### Yellow Leaves Don't Always Mean Overwatering

Overwatering is a common reason for yellow leaves in winter, but it isn't the only one.

Older leaves naturally age and die.

A plant may also shed foliage after a reduction in light, a sudden temperature change or root damage.

If one old leaf turns yellow while the rest of the plant remains healthy, there may be little to worry about.

If several leaves turn yellow rapidly and the soil stays wet, inspect the roots and drainage.

### Brown Tips Need a Different Investigation

Brown tips are often associated with dry air, but they can also result from irregular moisture or fertilizer salt buildup.

If multiple plants develop brown tips after heating begins, check indoor humidity.

If only one plant is affected, its potting mix, watering habits or species requirements may be more relevant.

### A Plant That Stops Growing Isn't Necessarily Unhealthy

It's normal for many houseplants to grow more slowly when light becomes limited.

Don't judge a plant's health only by how many new leaves it produces.

Firm stems, healthy roots and stable foliage are often more meaningful signs during winter.

The goal isn't to force continuous growth.

It's to provide suitable conditions until the plant becomes more active.

## A Simple Winter Houseplant Care Routine

You don't need to spend an hour examining every plant each day.

A short, consistent routine is usually more useful.

### Once a Week

Check soil moisture individually rather than watering every pot automatically.

Inspect leaf surfaces for pests and new damage.

Remove dead foliage when appropriate.

Check that no plant has been pushed too close to cold glass or a heater.

### Once a Month

Examine whether the amount of natural light has changed.

Wipe dusty leaves and clean windows if necessary.

Check room humidity, especially during periods of heavy heating.

Review whether any pots are staying wet for an unusually long time.

Adjust placement when there is a clear reason, rather than moving plants unnecessarily.

### When Spring Approaches

Watch for renewed growth.

As plants become more active, they may need water more frequently.

You can then consider resuming appropriate fertilization and carrying out necessary repotting.

Remember that stronger spring sunlight can also cause problems for plants that were moved close to windows during winter.

Reassess their positions as seasonal conditions change.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Winter Houseplant Care

### Should I water all my plants less during winter?

Not automatically.

Many houseplants need less frequent watering when light and growth decrease, but the correct approach depends on the species, temperature, humidity and potting mix.

Plants kept under grow lights or in warm, bright rooms may continue using water relatively quickly.

Always check the soil before watering.

### Why are my houseplant leaves turning yellow in winter?

Yellow leaves can result from excessive watering, insufficient light, temperature stress or normal aging.

Check whether the soil remains wet for long periods, whether the plant receives enough light and whether cold drafts are affecting it.

Don't assume that yellow leaves mean the plant needs fertilizer.

### Can houseplants survive winter without sunlight?

Plants need light for photosynthesis, but not necessarily direct sunlight.

Some tolerate relatively low light levels, although very dark conditions can eventually weaken them.

If natural light is extremely limited, appropriate artificial lighting may help.

### Should I mist my houseplants every day?

Daily misting generally isn't an effective substitute for maintaining suitable ambient humidity.

The increase in moisture is usually temporary.

For plants that genuinely require higher humidity, a humidifier and proper environmental management are more reliable options.

### Can I fertilize indoor plants in December or January?

It depends on whether the plants are actively growing.

Most houseplants experiencing reduced winter light need little or no additional fertilizer.

Plants growing vigorously under suitable lighting or species with winter growth cycles may have different requirements.

### Why is my houseplant losing leaves even though I water it?

Watering alone doesn't guarantee healthy roots.

Cold exposure, insufficient light, overwatering, root damage and environmental changes can all lead to leaf loss.

Check the soil, drainage, temperature and recent changes before deciding what to do.

### How often should I rotate my houseplants in winter?

There isn't a universal schedule. I also have no proper schedule for it, 

If a plant receives directional light and begins leaning, occasional rotation can encourage more balanced growth.

Plants with sensitive foliage or stable growth don't need constant repositioning.

### When should I start normal plant care again?

Increase watering and resume appropriate feeding when growing conditions improve and the plant shows signs of renewed activity.

For many indoor plants in temperate climates, this happens during spring.

However, the best timing depends on your actual indoor environment rather than a particular date.

## The Most Important Thing to Remember

The part of last winter I remember most clearly isn't the yellow leaves. It's how relieved I was when both plants started recovering after three or four days. I'd had my money plant and aloe vera for four years, and I genuinely thought I might lose them. A month later, both had recovered.

What stayed with me is how differently they were being kept: one indoors in water without direct sunlight, the other outdoors receiving sun. They looked as if they had the same problem, but that didn't mean they necessarily had the same cause. Getting individual advice helped me take action instead of guessing.

For most winter houseplants, the practical starting point is simple: check the soil or water, look at the roots, assess the light and temperature, and identify what's actually changed. Resist the urge to add extra water or fertilizer just because a leaf turns yellow. **Pay attention to the plant in front of you, not a universal schedule.**
